# Canonical Research Objective — Putnam U.S. Democracy Project

## Final Research Goal
The central purpose of this project is to determine empirically whether American democracy has undergone a gradual, long-term decline over roughly the last two decades, and to identify the relationship among democratic performance, Putnam-style social capital, and economic conditions.

This is NOT primarily a January 6 event-study and does not assume that a single political event caused democratic decline. The Trump era and January 6, 2021 are analytically relevant as possible breakpoints or interaction periods within a longer trajectory, not as predetermined monocausal explanations.

## Core Intellectual Strategy
1. Replicate and update Robert Putnam's social-capital measurement framework as faithfully as possible, preserving the original variables and operational definitions rather than replacing them with convenient modern proxies.
2. Apply the updated Putnam measures to the 50 U.S. states over 2000-2023 and construct both state-level and national longitudinal measures of social capital.
3. Measure the trajectory of democratic performance over the same period.
4. Add economic variables explicitly rather than treating economic conditions as external to the civic-democratic mechanism.
5. Test whether deterioration or change in economic conditions is associated with changes in social capital and whether social capital and economic conditions jointly explain variation in democratic performance.

## Central Theoretical Contribution
The project treats the omission or insufficient treatment of economic conditions in Putnam-style explanations of democratic performance as an empirical question and potential limitation to be tested, not as a conclusion assumed in advance.

A central possibility to test is:
Economic conditions -> Social Capital -> Democratic Performance

alongside direct and joint pathways:
Economic conditions -> Democratic Performance
Social Capital -> Democratic Performance
Economic conditions x Social Capital -> Democratic Performance

The empirical analysis must distinguish correlation, temporal ordering, mediation, interaction, and causal identification. It must not label a relationship causal unless the research design supports that inference.

## Core Research Questions
RQ1. Has U.S. democratic performance gradually declined over 2000-2023?
RQ2. Has Putnam-style social capital declined over the same period, and how closely does its trajectory correspond to democratic performance?
RQ3. How have state and national economic conditions changed over the same period?
RQ4. To what extent are economic conditions associated with social-capital levels and changes?
RQ5. Do social capital and economic conditions independently, jointly, or sequentially account for variation in democratic performance?
RQ6. Does adding economic variables materially change the estimated relationship between Putnam-style social capital and democratic performance?
RQ7. Are the Trump era and the post-January-6 period statistically distinguishable from the longer-term trajectory after accounting for pre-existing social-capital and economic trends?

## Empirical Architecture
Primary panel: 50 U.S. states x 2000-2023 = 1,200 state-years.
Primary democracy DV: Berkeley State Democracy Index 2.0.
Primary social-capital IV: faithfully updated Putnam-style social-capital measures.
Economic block: to be specified with theoretically justified state-year measures of economic performance, distribution, insecurity, and structural change.
External democracy validation: V-Dem where appropriate.
No interpolation of canonical missing observations.

## Interpretation Principle
The research must allow the data to reject the decline thesis. Long-run democratic decline, social-capital decline, economic deterioration, mediation, and structural breaks are hypotheses to be tested rather than findings to be assumed.

## Intended Scholarly Contribution
The project aims to make two linked contributions:
1. provide a longitudinal empirical assessment of the trajectory and determinants of contemporary U.S. democratic performance; and
2. reassess the explanatory sufficiency of Putnam's social-capital framework by testing whether explicit economic variables add explanatory power, alter the social-capital coefficient, mediate social-capital formation, or reveal a broader political-economy mechanism connecting economic change, civic life, and democratic performance.

This document is the canonical research-objective statement. Subsequent abstracts, research designs, variable selection, models, and interpretation should remain consistent with it unless explicitly revised.
